When I worked as a Corporate Trainer and Instructional Designer in the pharmaceutical industry outside Philadelphia, training was a big deal. Pharma companies invested heavily — traveling trainers like me to sales reps, custom CRM training, comprehensive onboarding programs. It was expensive and it was worth it.

Later, working for a mortgage software company, our clients paid for custom instructional documentation I wrote. High-quality training materials were a product. Organizations understood: good training = better adoption = better outcomes.

Those days seem largely gone.

Today, organizations buy expensive software and ship it with minimal documentation. They expect users to figure it out. Training budgets have shrunk. Instructional designers are a rarity. “Self-service” and YouTube tutorials have replaced structured, professionally-written instruction.

But here’s what I’m wondering: did cutting training budgets actually save money? Or did it just hide the cost?

**The Shift: What Changed?**

Then (2000s-2010s):

Pharma invested in sales training because rep productivity directly impacted revenue
Software companies sold custom training as a premium product
Organizations understood ROI: good training = faster adoption = faster productivity = profit
Training was a strategic investment, not an expense

Now (2020s):

Training budgets are slashed or eliminated
“Documentation” often means auto-generated system help or YouTube videos
Organizations expect users to self-teach
Training is treated as a cost center to be minimized

**Why the shift?**

Cloud and SaaS changed the economics — Updates are constant, training content becomes outdated quickly. Organizations figured “why invest if it changes monthly?”
The rise of self-service culture — “Millennials figured it out on YouTube, so everyone should be able to.” The assumption is that people will Google answers instead of relying on formal training.
Cost-cutting mentality — Training is visible, expensive, easy to cut. Nobody measures the hidden cost of poor adoption or support burden.
Misunderstanding of knowledge work — Organizations treated training like a one-time event instead of ongoing support. When the training didn’t stick because it was poorly designed, they blamed the learner, not the training.
The Hidden Cost No One’s Measuring

Here’s what I’ve observed in warehouse operations and ERP implementations:

Organizations cut training budgets by 50%
Adoption rates drop (people don’t use features they don’t understand)
Support tickets spike (people asking basic questions)
Implementation timelines extend (ramp-up takes 3x longer)
Errors increase (people guess at processes)
But nobody connects these dots back to the training cuts. They blame “user resistance” or “poor system design” instead of recognizing: we didn’t invest in helping people learn it.

**Why Pharma Got It Right**

Pharmaceutical companies understood something we seem to have forgotten: training is how you achieve business outcomes.

For sales reps, better training meant:

* Faster time-to-productivity (they could sell sooner)
* Higher adoption of CRM tools (more deals tracked)
* Better client relationships (more informed reps)
* Measurable revenue impact

They measured ROI because the connection was obvious. Training → adoption → productivity → revenue.

We’ve lost that thinking in other industries.

**The Question I’m Asking**

Did organizations actually save money by cutting training? Or did they just stop measuring the cost?

I’d argue the latter. The cost is still there — it’s just hidden in:

* Support tickets (expensive to answer)
* Rework (people make mistakes because they don’t understand the process)
* Lost productivity (people spend time figuring out what they should have been trained on)
* Implementation delays (projects take longer because adoption is slower)
* Employee frustration (people avoid systems they don’t understand)

**What would happen if we actually measured that?**

What I’d Like to See Change

Organizations need to rediscover what pharma companies already knew:

* Training is not an expense — it’s an investment with measurable ROI
* Well-designed instruction matters (not just pretty, but structurally sound)
* Learning takes time (one onboarding session doesn’t stick; ongoing support matters)
* Good training enables adoption, which enables the outcomes you’re paying for the system to deliver

**Closing thought:**

The irony is that organizations are paying millions for software they’re not equipping people to use well. Then they blame the software for poor adoption.

They used to get this right. Pharmaceutical companies did. Mortgage software companies did.

I think it’s time to remember why.]
