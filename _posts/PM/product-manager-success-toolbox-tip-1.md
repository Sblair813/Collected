Have you ever sat in a meeting where the PM talks too much and doesn't listen? You're not alone. I've sat through plenty of them, and they rarely produce results.
Many PMs feel they need to facilitate or run the meeting, and sometimes they're right. But when the goal is to understand a request, a problem, or stakeholder needs, PMs need to take a back seat and listen.
The cost of not listening is steep: missed insights, wrong solutions, and wasted time.

---

The Problem: Generic Problem Statements Hide the Real Story
Stakeholders come to you with a problem statement: "We need a  button that resets the pick."
That's the headline, but it's not the full story.
If a PM doesn't listen (really listen), they'll fixate on the problem statement and miss the mark. They'll build what they think is needed instead of what's actually needed.
The golden nuggets are in the fringe conversations: the asides, the seemingly tangential comments, the details stakeholders mention in passing. But those insights only surface when the PM stops talking and listens.

---

Real Example: Problem Orders
I was invited to a meeting to find a way to cancel bad orders that had been downloaded into the warehouse system. I understood these orders were causing errors in the warehouse's systems and processes. I listened, heard their pain points from the warehouse perspective, and took detailed notes.
Please note that canceling an order past a certain stage involves multiple systems - particularly if a shipping label has already been generated. Additionally, labor resources have already been utilized for fulfillment.
I then sat down with Customer Service, the team that actually handles these orders. Again, I listened.
What I learned by listening:
The specific details that matter to them when researching these orders
How they currently investigate suspicious orders
The manual workarounds they use in the backend
Problem orders delay order delivery, especially during high volume times
Sending an email to the CS group gets lost in a sea of email and is ineffective, everyone thinks someone else addressed the issue

None of that was in the original problem statement. I learned it only by asking questions and, more importantly, by not interrupting when they went off on tangents.
Aiming to intercept these orders before they enter the download or fulfillment pipeline, I reviewed the allocation process and partnered with engineering to explore and validate potential solutions.
The Solution
After building several prototypes, I met with Customer Service again to refine the design. They loved the direction and offered key operational insights.
To streamline their workflow, I developed an actionable report that included the specific data columns CS needed to investigate orders quickly. This allowed them to easily sort data and spot patterns, such as multiple high-risk orders targeting the same address. By integrating a release function directly into the report, I eliminated a manual, multi-step process, allowing CS to evaluate suspicious orders and release legitimate ones - all from a single dashboard.
The Impact
CS no longer had to toggle between systems
Legitimate customer orders were released faster, reducing customer complaints
CS spent significantly less time on manual backend work
The problem order investigation process became streamlined
The warehouse associates weren't delayed by errors the problem orders were causing in their systems

Had I not actively gathered user feedback, I would have delivered only a costly cancellation feature in the warehouse system - leaving Customer Service tied to manual workarounds in a secondary system and creating delays for end customers. Active collaboration ensured a complete end-to-end solution rather than a partial fix.

---

Why Listening Matters (More Than You Think)
1. You discover the real problem, not the symptom.
 Stakeholders describe the symptom. Listening reveals the root cause, and that changes everything about what you build.
2. You catch details that would take months of discovery to uncover.
 Those fringe comments are gold. A casual mention like "we spend 30 minutes a day on manual releases" is worth more than a formal requirements document, because it changes how you prioritize.
3. You build trust.
 When stakeholders feel heard, they trust you more. They bring you problems earlier and believe you'll actually solve them. That trust becomes your greatest asset as a PM.
4. You save time.
 Listening upfront saves months of wasted development, rework, and rediscovery. You get it right the first time because you truly understood what was needed.
5. Stakeholders feel valued.
 When a PM takes the time to really listen, not just wait for their turn to talk, stakeholders feel respected. That matters more than you think, and it changes the entire dynamic of how you work together.

---

The Red Flags: When a PM Isn't Listening
Interrupting stakeholders when they go off-topic (that "off-topic" comment might be the insight you need)
Rushing through conversations to stick to an agenda
Talking more than asking questions
Building solutions before fully understanding the problem
Not asking follow-up questions when something doesn't make sense
Assuming you know what stakeholders need without asking

If you catch yourself doing any of these, stop. Take a breath. Ask another question instead.

---

The Bottom Line
Being a PM doesn't mean managing every conversation. It means understanding problems deeply enough to solve them well.
Listening isn't passive. It's the most strategic thing you can do.
The best PMs I know aren't the ones with all the answers. They're the ones who ask the right questions and actually listen to the answers. They create space for stakeholders to think out loud, and they catch the golden nuggets in the fringe conversations.
When you listen, stakeholders feel heard. When stakeholders feel heard, they trust you. And when they trust you, they bring you better problems, and you build better solutions.
Rome wasn't built in a day, and neither are good products. You're better off taking the time to listen and understand now than rushing to solutions later.
